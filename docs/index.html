Will AI take radiology jobs?

We simulated 20,000 possible futures of the U.S. radiologist workforce, modeling imaging demand, how much time AI saves, regulation, the new imaging AI creates, and the training pipeline. The forecast is for anyone considering, training in, or practicing diagnostic radiology.

Key terms

Medians, ranges and P(X)

Every number comes from the of simulated futures not already contradicted by events. The median is the middle value; the 80% range holds the middle 80% (10th to 90th percentile). P(X), or "chance of X", is the share of futures in which X happens in that year.

Demand and supply (FTEs)

Demand: full-time-equivalent radiologists (FTEs) needed to read the imaging patients and doctors want from radiologists, given the AI in use and after the market adjustment (during a shortage demand drifts down toward supply; during a surplus, up). Supply: practicing radiologists × the hours each works. Both are indexed so 2026 demand = 1.00, the work of about full-time radiologists at today's hours; about practice today, so supply ≈ .

Supply ÷ demand

The single best summary of the job market: below 1 is a shortage, above 1 a surplus. We estimate about today (80% range –), combining pay, vacancies and workload with a reconstruction of the job market since 1995; no measured national figure exists.

Meaningful oversupply and shortage

Supply ÷ demand above 1.10 (at least 10% more radiologists than the work needs) or below 0.90, a convention for a clearly noticeable imbalance. For scale, our reconstruction puts the 2012–13 market, when new graduates struggled to find jobs, at about , so meaningful oversupply is about twice that surplus. It is a pressure indicator, not an unemployment rate.

Market adjustment

In effect it pulls supply ÷ demand back toward 1, but only so far. When radiologists are scarce, other doctors take on more of the reading and labor-saving tools spread faster; the model assumes the reverse when radiologists are plentiful. Each year about of a gap closes (80% range –), a speed fitted to radiology's job market since 1995. History shows the market can absorb a gap of at least about 10% of demand; our central limit of about (range –) is partly judgment, and the evidence that surpluses are absorbed rests mostly on one recovery, 2013–2018 (details). Demand figures include it.

AI-assisted vs AI-first reading

AI-assisted: the radiologist reads; AI drafts, measures and flags. AI-first: AI reads and reports first; a radiologist audits, signs or handles escalations, which in the main model still takes about a fifth of the usual time on average (spot checks, escalations and sign-off, not a full review of every case). AI time saved combines both (saving a third of the time = 1.5× as much work per hour).

AI-progress regimes

How fast general AI improves is the largest unknown, so each future falls into one of four regimes: Stall ( of futures: radiology AI takes 1.6–3 times as long as under Trend), Trend (: today's pace), Fast (: about twice as fast), and Transformative AI (: AI eventually does nearly all of a radiologist's cognitive work). The weights are judgment calls, shown after removing futures already contradicted by events; change them in the explorer.

2026 · Today

A specialty that was supposed to be automated is short of people

In 2016 Geoffrey Hinton, a pioneer of deep learning, said we should stop training radiologists because AI would soon outperform them. Ten years later, diagnostic-radiology programs offered a record 1,241 positions and filled 97.6% of them. Average pay rose 6.6% in a year, and workforce projections still show a shortage a decade out.

AI has not gone away. More than 1,600 AI devices are FDA-listed, about three-quarters in radiology, but few are used at scale. Measured time savings range from none to 42% depending on the task and the study.§

Starting point: supply ÷ demand ≈ (we estimate a shortage of about ), AI saving almost no radiologist time yet, and about practicing radiologists.

That is well below the one-third that a leading task-based analysis calls the five-year potential, because adopting tools takes years even after they work.§

How a surplus or shortage would be felt: pay and hiring

This section translates the supply ÷ demand forecast into pay and hiring, using how radiologists' pay moved in past shortages and surpluses. It is not a separate forecast of wages. Radiologists' pay rose faster than other physicians' while they were scarce (2001–2006, 2022–2025) and fell behind while they were plentiful (2009–2014). Fitted to 2001–2014, that pattern is consistent with the 2022–2025 pay surge, but only after we recoded the earlier pay data, and it rests on a few mild imbalances, so treat these figures as rough.§

Why: AI time savings and the regulatory pipeline

Tiers of exam difficulty. Interpretive work is split into four tiers that AI could take over one after another: 1 normal radiographs and negative screens (≈7% of interpretive work), 2 other radiographs and screening (≈17%), 3 most CT, MRI, ultrasound and nuclear medicine (≈47%), 4 the hardest remaining work (≈29%). The grouping is our own, not a standard classification (why), so we also test automation in any order.

Being able to read a study is not the same as being allowed and paid to read it. For AI-first reading of each tier, the model chains: technical capability → clinical validation → FDA authorization → liability and payment → hospital adoption → actual time saved.§

What can today's AI do compared with radiologists, and what can't it? Two good overviews: a review of AI image interpretation in the New England Journal of Medicine and an accessible essay on why AI has not replaced radiologists. The full report summarizes the 2024–2026 studies.

Why: the Jevons question

Could cheaper, faster AI reads create so much new imaging that radiologists end up busier? Economists call that a Jevons paradox: making something more efficient raises total use of it, as with coal in 19th-century Britain. In the median future, new work offsets about of the time AI saves, and exceeds the saving in only of futures by 2045.§ That depends on new uses nobody can yet measure, such as image-derived biomarkers: with three times as many new imaging uses and twice the new radiologist tasks, the chance that new work exceeds the time saved rises from to (report §8.4).

Why cheaper reads don't create much demand

The radiologist's fee is only ~20–25% of an exam's price, and imaging demand barely responds to price (a 10% cut in the patient's out-of-pocket price raises use by about 2%, and most of a fee cut would fall on payers, not patients). So halving interpretation cost adds only about 1–2% more exams.

The bigger channels are new uses (opportunistic screening on CTs done for other reasons, raising lung-screening uptake from 18%), new radiologist tasks, faster scanners and faster turnaround. AI also adds some radiologist work, such as oversight and follow-up of incidental findings, which the model includes. Technologist shortages cap the extra exams.

Why: the market adjusts

Training a radiologist takes years, but radiology's job market has repeatedly closed shortages and surpluses within a few years: other doctors read more while radiologists were scarce, and labor-saving tools spread faster. Version 1.6 adds this market adjustment and tests it against radiology's own history.

Testing against radiology's own history

We reconstructed radiology's job market from 1995 to 2026 from published growth in imaging, productivity and the workforce, and compared it with eight documented episodes, such as the mid-1990s glut, the 2000 shortage, the 2012–13 surplus for new graduates and today's shortage, each recorded at the time in job ads and workforce surveys. To make the test fair, we tuned the model only on 1995–2013, then checked whether it predicted 2015–2025 without having seen those years (we did set the input ranges in 2026, knowing what happened, so the test is not perfectly blind).§

Solid boxes were used for tuning; dashed boxes were kept aside as the test. Hover over a box for the probability each version gave it.

What we learned. The earlier model tracked supply and demand with no way for the market to respond. It matched 1995–2013 but predicted a surplus through the 2010s; in fact the market recovered by 2016 and was short by 2022.

History supports:

  • a market adjustment that is at least moderately fast and absorbs gaps of at least about 10% of demand;
  • tuned only on 1995–2013, it made the error on the test years less than half as large, mostly by pulling the market back toward balance.

History can't tell us:

  • how fast or large the adjustment could get, because past gaps never exceeded about 10–15%; the upper ends we use are judgments;
  • whether surpluses are really absorbed: that rests mostly on one recovery (2013–2018), and how work flows back to radiologists is assumed. If they are not, the 2045 oversupply chance is .

One caution: on the test years, our forecasts did worse than two naive guesses, a balanced market and no change from the last known state (there were only three test episodes). Those guesses can say nothing about AI, which is why we model it, but treat the long-range numbers as reasoned judgment, not a proven track record (full test).

What this changed

Version 1.5 put the 2045 oversupply chance at 42%. Three changes driven by this history bring it to :

  • recent imaging growth per person rises from 0.6% to 1.2% a year, between v1.5's claims-based estimate and the 1.8% the history implies, with a wider range, because wider ranges predicted past years better;
  • today's shortage is slightly milder, as reconstructed;
  • the market adjustment is added.

How the model works

Five pieces are modeled separately and then linked, with a market adjustment that closes part of any gap between supply and demand. Each arrow carries uncertain inputs drawn from probability distributions, and the pieces are correlated: faster AI raises time savings and creates new imaging uses and speeds up scanners. Methods in brief

The "§" links above point here. The full report has every equation, table and source. We simulated futures and kept the not already contradicted by events (such as an FDA-authorized autonomous radiology read before now); with that many, simulation noise is under half a point.

Baseline demand

Radiologist work grows with demographics (+0.52%/yr, from projections adjusted for lower immigration), imaging per person (starting near %/yr and slowing, combining recent claims-based trends with the history reconstruction) and work per exam (0.4%/yr, slowing), minus up to 15% displaced by blood tests and other alternatives. Today's shortage (median , 80% range –) combines workforce projections and market signals with the history reconstruction. Report §4.1

AI time savings

Radiologist time is split into five tasks: interpretation 42%, drafting and measurement 18%, consultation 13%, administration 15%, procedures 12%. Each has an uncertain maximum AI time saving, a capability curve and an adoption curve. Maximums are anchored on measured effects: 15.5% faster documentation in live use, 42% faster reading in a reader study, and no significant pooled saving in real-world deployments. AI also adds oversight work. Adoption speeds up while there is a shortage. Report §4.2

Regulation and adoption

Each of the four tiers must pass validation, FDA authorization, and liability and payment acceptance before AI-first reading can begin, and an AI-first study still needs about a fifth of the radiologist's time for audit and sign-off (far less in the transformative-AI regime). Lags are anchored on MASAI (~5 years), the scarcity of clinical validation, autonomous retinal AI (3 years from FDA to payment), liability research, and the spread of computer-aided detection and electronic health records. Tier sizes come from autonomous-AI studies. Report §4.3

Jevons accounting

New work created by AI = cheaper reads (price elasticity ≈ −0.2 × fee share ≈ 20%) + faster turnaround + scanner throughput + new uses + follow-up of AI-found findings + new radiologist tasks − AI utilization management − reads shifting to other doctors, capped by scanner capacity. Time saved = baseline work × share of time AI saves. A Jevons paradox means new work exceeds time saved. Report §4.4

Supply

A cohort model tracks radiologists by years in practice (mean career ≈ 35 years; measured attrition 2.0–2.5%/yr in 2019–2022), calibrated so that flat residency positions reproduce the Neiman Institute's +25.7% by 2055. Positions (1,241 in 2026) grow about %/yr, a little below the 2010–2025 pace, and react to the market with a lag. New residents enter practice ~6 years after matching. Report §4.5

Market adjustment

Each year about of the remaining gap between supply and demand closes (80% range –) as work shifts between radiologists and other doctors and labor-saving tools spread faster or slower, up to a limit of about of demand (–). History bounds both only from below (fitted on 1995–2013, checked on 2015–2025); the upper ends are judgments (history test). Beyond the limit, a gap persists until residency positions respond. Report §4.7

Pay readout

Radiologist pay relative to other physicians changes each year in proportion to the imbalance (β ≈ – per unit of log supply ÷ demand; fitted to 2001–2014 and refitted on all years; it matches 2022–2025 only after we recoded the earlier pay data). Each year it also closes 5–20% of its gap to a normal level between today's and about 15% below it, before the recent shortage premium (both judgments). Imbalances beyond ±5% are treated as ±5%, since the fit saw only small ones. It does not feed back into supply or demand. Report §9.2

Uncertainty and regimes

uncertain inputs are drawn from explicit distributions and correlated through three shared factors: AI progress, regulatory friction and appetite for imaging. The AI factor picks one of the four regimes. Each input is graded anchored or subjective (see all). Report §4.6

Validation

The supply model reproduces published projections, unit tests check the accounting identities, the model is tested against radiology's job market since 1995 with a time split between fitting and held-out years, and a 2016→2025 backtest of three other jobs compares a simplified version of the method with official projections and trends. Report §6

Sensitivity analysis

Alternative assumptions and model designs (the table under How sure?) and, in the report, a tornado chart that pins groups of inputs at their 10th and 90th percentiles, variance shares and evidence-class attribution. Report §8

Every assumption, graded

Each input, its distribution and percentiles, and where it comes from. A anchored on data plus judgment, S subjective. Be most skeptical of the S rows: they account for most of the uncertainty.
