Will AI take radiology jobs?

We simulated 20,000 possible futures of the U.S. radiologist workforce, modeling imaging demand, how much time AI saves, regulation, the new imaging AI creates, and the training pipeline. The forecast is for anyone considering, training in, or practicing diagnostic radiology.

The short answer

Probably not in the next decade. Over a career there is a real chance, about by 2045, of at least 10% more radiologists than the work needs, mostly because AI lets each radiologist do more.

The next decade. The market will most likely stay balanced or short, which is good for job seekers. In 2035, meaningful oversupply () and meaningful shortage () are about equally likely.§

Why. Imaging keeps growing, but demand for radiologists barely does, because AI absorbs the growth. In the median future in 2045:

  1. With no further radiology AI, radiologist work would be higher than today
    An older population, more imaging per person and bigger studies.§
  2. AI saves of radiologist time
    Drafting and measurement first, then AI-first reading of simpler exams as regulation and payment allow (details).
  3. New work AI creates gives back about of that saving
    So demand for radiologists ends up only above today (details).
  4. Supply grows from about below today's demand to about above it
    Training takes about six years after medical school, about 2.5% of radiologists leave each year, and residency positions keep growing and respond to a surplus only after several years.§

The risk peaks in the 2050s ( in 2055), then eases a little as residency programs adjust. Most of it comes from AI (how much), and AI is unlikely to create more radiologist work than it saves (why).

What it means. People entering practice in the next decade will most likely start in a balanced or short market. For radiologists already in practice, the main effects are a changing job and, if oversupply comes, fewer openings and flatter pay. By career stage →

How sure. Trust the direction more than the digits. The percentages show what follows from our assumptions; they are not odds with a track record. Key terms

Medians, ranges and P(X)

Every number comes from simulated futures. The median is the middle value; the 80% range holds the middle 80% (10th to 90th percentile). Each median is taken separately, so median supply ÷ median demand can differ slightly from the median ratio. P(X), or "chance of X", is the share of futures in which X happens.

Demand and supply (FTEs)

Demand: full-time-equivalent radiologists (FTEs) needed to read the imaging patients and doctors want, given the AI in use. Supply: practicing radiologists × the hours each works. Both are indexed so 2026 demand = 1.00, the work of about full-time radiologists at today's hours; about practice today, so supply ≈ .

Supply ÷ demand

The single best summary of the job market: below 1 is a shortage, above 1 a surplus. We estimate about today (80% range –), a judgment from pay, vacancies and workload, since no measured national figure exists.

Meaningful oversupply and shortage

Supply ÷ demand above 1.10 (at least 10% more radiologists than the work needs) or below 0.90. The thresholds are a convention for a clearly noticeable imbalance; the mid-1990s and mid-2010s gluts, when new graduates struggled to find jobs, were never measured on this scale. It is a pressure indicator, not an unemployment rate.

AI-assisted vs AI-first reading

AI-assisted: the radiologist reads; AI drafts, measures and flags. AI-first: AI reads and reports first; a radiologist audits, signs or handles escalations, which in the main model still takes about a fifth of the usual time on average (spot checks, escalations and sign-off, not a full review of every case). AI time saved combines both (saving a third of the time = 1.5× as much work per hour).

AI-progress regimes

How fast general AI improves is the largest unknown, so each future falls into one of four regimes: Stall ( of futures: radiology AI takes 1.6–3 times as long as under Trend), Trend (: today's pace), Fast (: about twice as fast), and Transformative AI (: AI eventually does nearly all of a radiologist's cognitive work). The weights are judgment calls (set at 15/55/18/12 before we dropped futures already contradicted by events); change them in the explorer.

Tiers of exam difficulty

Four groups of exams that AI could read first, one after another: 1 normal radiographs and negative screens, 2 other radiographs and screening, 3 most CT and MRI, 4 the hardest remaining work (details).

2026 · Today

A specialty that was supposed to be automated is short of people

In 2016 Geoffrey Hinton, a pioneer of deep learning, said we should stop training radiologists because AI would soon outperform them. Ten years later, diagnostic-radiology programs offered a record 1,241 positions and filled 97.6% of them. Average pay rose 6.6% in a year, and workforce projections still show a shortage a decade out.

AI has not gone away. More than 1,600 AI devices are FDA-listed, about three-quarters in radiology, but few are used at scale. Measured time savings range from none to 42% depending on the task and the study.§

Starting point: supply ÷ demand ≈ (we estimate a shortage of about ), AI saving almost no radiologist time yet, and about practicing radiologists.

Oversupply is near zero for anyone starting before about 2030, because the model starts from a shortage. That shortage (about 7%) is mostly smaller than the 10% cutoff for "meaningful", so meaningful shortage at the start is modest. Each figure can move by roughly 15–25 points under other defensible assumptions (How sure?).

Already practicing? Your horizon is the years to retirement. About 2.5% of radiologists retire or leave each year, so a slow fall in demand is absorbed by hiring fewer new graduates. Demand falls faster than that 2.5% a year at some point in of futures, nearly all with transformative AI. But new graduates keep arriving, so oversupply can grow even when demand is flat: severe oversupply (25% or more too many radiologists) lasting five or more years occurs in of futures.

Where would AI risk show up?

The model forecasts only supply ÷ demand. How a surplus splits between fewer openings, flatter pay, shorter hours or payers capturing the gains is not modeled; the items below are interpretations from past gluts and labor economics.

  1. What the job involvesfollows most directly from our task assumptions
    Drafting, measurement and protocoling shrink; consultation, procedures and AI oversight grow (see How a radiologist's day changes).
  2. Workload intensitylikely while short
    In shortage futures, time saved becomes more studies per hour rather than shorter days.
  3. Hiring of new graduatesfirst to be affected in surplus futures
    Fewer openings and residency cuts, as in the mid-1990s and mid-2010s. For scale: absorbing a 15% surplus by hiring alone would mean roughly halving new-graduate entry for about years, after a training lag of about six years. In other AI-exposed jobs, early-career employment fell first.
  4. Pay and hourslikely follow supply ÷ demand
    Shortage-driven pay growth would likely flatten or reverse once supply exceeds demand, though reimbursement changes could shift gains to hospitals or insurers. Absorbing a 15% surplus by hours alone would mean about fewer hours each.

Skills that are harder to automate, by assumption. The model assumes procedures (median AI time-saving ceiling ≈7%) and consultation (≈24%) are the least automatable tasks; this is an input, not a finding.

Why: AI time savings and the regulatory pipeline

Tiers of exam difficulty. Interpretive work is split into four tiers that AI could take over one after another: 1 normal radiographs and negative screens (≈7% of interpretive work), 2 other radiographs and screening (≈17%), 3 most CT, MRI, ultrasound and nuclear medicine (≈47%), 4 the hardest remaining work (≈29%). The grouping is our own, not a standard classification (why), so we also test automation in any order.

Being able to read a study is not the same as being allowed and paid to read it. For AI-first reading of each tier, the model chains: technical capability → clinical validation → FDA authorization → liability and payment → hospital adoption → actual time saved.§

Why: the Jevons question

Could cheaper, faster AI reads create so much new imaging that radiologists end up busier? Economists call that a Jevons paradox: making something more efficient raises total use of it, as with coal in 19th-century Britain. In the median future, new work offsets about of the time AI saves, and exceeds the saving in only of futures by 2045.§ That depends on new uses nobody can yet measure, such as image-derived biomarkers: with three times as many new imaging uses and twice the new radiologist tasks, the chance that new work exceeds the time saved rises from to (report §8.4).

How much of the saving is offset?
New work ÷ time saved: median and 80% range. Above 1 = a true Jevons paradox.

Why cheaper reads don't create much demand

The radiologist's fee is only ~20–25% of an exam's price, and imaging demand barely responds to price (a 10% cut in the patient's out-of-pocket price raises use by about 2%, and most of a fee cut would fall on payers, not patients). So halving interpretation cost adds only about 1–2% more exams.

The bigger channels are new uses (opportunistic screening on CTs done for other reasons, raising lung-screening uptake from 18%), new radiologist tasks, faster scanners and faster turnaround. AI also adds some radiologist work, such as oversight and follow-up of incidental findings, which the model includes. Technologist shortages cap the extra exams.

How sure?

With futures, simulation noise is under half a point. (We drew and dropped those already contradicted by events, such as an FDA-authorized autonomous radiology read before now.) The real uncertainty is in our assumptions and model design, so the table re-runs the forecast under other defensible choices.

What holds and what doesn't. The direction holds in every row: oversupply risk is lower in 2035 than later and rises over a career. The exact numbers do not: the 2045 figure ranges from to , mostly because of assumptions about AI speed and imaging growth, which matter far more than anything else. These ranges cover a chosen set of alternatives, not every plausible future.

How much of the risk is AI? With no further radiology AI, today's shortage would mostly deepen (a meaningful shortage in of futures in 2045), and oversupply would occur only where imaging grows slowly ( in 2045, in 2055). Near-term risk comes almost entirely from assistive tools, mostly in the transformative regime, where their extra time savings bypass regulation. By 2055 about half the risk would exist even without further radiology AI, and AI-first reading adds most of the rest.

The least-measured input is the starting point. Today's balance (≈) is a judgment. A rough check: practicing radiologists grew only about 1% a year in 2010–2022, and if imaging work grew 1.5–2.5% a year, a market near balance or in slight surplus in 2016 would sit around 0.85–0.99 today. That is consistent with 0.93 but cannot rule out balance: with no shortage today, the 2045 oversupply chance would be (row above).

A backtest

Run from 2016 for three automation-exposed jobs, a simplified version of this method put every 2025 outcome inside its ranges, but a simple average of the official Bureau of Labor Statistics projection and the past trend was more accurate: our AI adjustment over-predicted job losses in medical transcription. Run from 2016 for radiology, it gave about a 2-in-3 chance (– across variants) of the shortage we estimate today. It is a sanity check, not proof that a 40-year forecast is accurate (full backtest; why a structured model).§

What would change the forecast

If we observe X, how likely is meaningful oversupply? Some signals arrive within a few years, others later. Each row shows the chance of meaningful oversupply in the futures where that signal occurs; the small figure is how often the signal occurs.

These are signals, not levers. Early payment for AI-first reads mostly tells you that AI is moving fast, and fast AI is what raises the risk; on its own, faster regulation changes the 2045 figure much less (report §8.1).

How the model works

Five pieces are modeled separately and then linked. Each arrow carries uncertain inputs drawn from probability distributions, and the pieces are correlated: faster AI raises time savings and creates new imaging uses and speeds up scanners. Click a box for details.

Methods in brief

The "§" links above point here. The full report has every equation, table and source.

Baseline demand

Radiologist work grows with demographics (+0.52%/yr, from projections adjusted for lower immigration), imaging per person (starting near %/yr and slowing, a judgment above recent claims-based trends) and work per exam (0.4%/yr, slowing), minus up to 15% displaced by blood tests and other alternatives. Today's shortage (median , 80% range –) is a judgment from workforce projections and market signals. Report §4.1

AI time savings

Radiologist time is split into five tasks: interpretation 42%, drafting and measurement 18%, consultation 13%, administration 15%, procedures 12%. Each has an uncertain maximum AI time saving, a capability curve and an adoption curve. Maximums are anchored on measured effects: 15.5% faster documentation in live use, 42% faster reading in a reader study, and no significant pooled saving in real-world deployments. AI also adds oversight work. Adoption speeds up while there is a shortage. Report §4.2

Regulation and adoption

Each of the four tiers must pass validation, FDA authorization, and liability and payment acceptance before AI-first reading can begin, and an AI-first study still needs about a fifth of the radiologist's time for audit and sign-off (far less in the transformative-AI regime). Lags are anchored on MASAI (~5 years), the scarcity of clinical validation, autonomous retinal AI (3 years from FDA to payment), liability research, and the spread of computer-aided detection and electronic health records. Tier sizes come from autonomous-AI studies. Report §4.3

Jevons accounting

New work created by AI = cheaper reads (price elasticity ≈ −0.2 × fee share ≈ 20%) + faster turnaround + scanner throughput + new uses + follow-up of AI-found findings + new radiologist tasks − AI utilization management − reads shifting to other doctors, capped by scanner capacity. Time saved = baseline work × share of time AI saves. A Jevons paradox means new work exceeds time saved. Report §4.4

Supply

A cohort model tracks radiologists by years in practice (mean career ≈ 35 years; measured attrition 2.0–2.5%/yr in 2019–2022), calibrated so that flat residency positions reproduce the Neiman Institute's +25.7% by 2055. Positions (1,241 in 2026) grow about %/yr, a little below the 2010–2025 pace, and react to the market with a lag. New residents enter practice ~6 years after matching. Report §4.5

Uncertainty and regimes

uncertain inputs are drawn from explicit distributions and correlated through three shared factors: AI progress, regulatory friction and appetite for imaging. The AI factor picks one of the four regimes. Each input is graded anchored or subjective (see all). Report §4.6

Validation

The supply model reproduces published projections, unit tests check the accounting identities, and a 2016→2025 backtest compares a simplified version of the method with official projections, trend extrapolation and their average. Report §6

Sensitivity analysis

Alternative assumptions and model designs (the table under How sure?) and, in the report, a tornado chart that pins groups of inputs at their 10th and 90th percentiles, variance shares and evidence-class attribution. Report §8

Every assumption, graded

Each input, its distribution and percentiles, and where it comes from. A anchored on data plus judgment, S subjective. Be most skeptical of the S rows: they account for most of the uncertainty.
