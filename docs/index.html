The short answer

The job market when you finish residency will probably still be good. The radiologists who will practice in 2035 have mostly already been trained, and imaging demand keeps rising as the population ages. In our median world the supply/demand ratio in 2035 is (below 1 means a shortage). There is a chance you graduate into a shortage and a chance of meaningful oversupply.

The risk is back-loaded, with a fat tail. As AI moves from drafting reports to reading studies on its own, the probability of oversupply climbs to ten years into your career and twenty years in. Demand collapsing below half of today's level is a ~ tail, almost entirely in worlds where AI becomes transformative.

"AI creates more imaging than it automates" is unlikely to hold for radiologists. Cheaper and faster reads, new screening programs and AI-found incidental findings do generate more work, but they offset only about half of the labor AI saves, and exceed it in fewer than 1 in 20 simulated worlds.

2026 · You are here

A specialty that was supposed to be automated is short of people

In 2016 a founder of deep learning told an audience to stop training radiologists, predicting that within five years AI would outperform them. Ten years later, diagnostic-radiology programs offered a record 1,241 positions and filled 97.6% of them. Average pay rose 6.6% in a year, and federal workforce projections still show a shortage a decade out.

AI has not gone away. The FDA lists more than 1,600 AI-enabled devices, about three-quarters of them in radiology. Yet insurance claims show that only a handful are used at scale, and a meta-analysis of real-world deployments found no significant time savings. Generative AI that drafts radiograph reports did cut reporting time 15.5% in one large health system, with no loss of accuracy.

The model starts here: a market short by about 7%, AI that saves almost no radiologist time yet, and roughly practicing radiologists.

The Jevons question

When coal engines became more efficient, Britain burned more coal. Could cheaper, faster AI reads likewise create so much new imaging that radiologists end up busier? We add up every channel separately, net of the countervailing ones.

Why cheaper reads don't create much demand

The radiologist's interpretation fee is only about 10%–30% of the all-in price of a scan. Imaging demand is price-inelastic (≈ −0.2). Halving interpretation cost therefore adds only ~1–2% more exams.

The bigger channels are new applications (opportunistic CT screening, lung screening uptake of 18%), new radiologist tasks, scanner throughput and faster turnaround. They are capped by technologist shortages (CT vacancy rate 19.4%).

In Bessen's terms, radiology looks like a mature market where demand is no longer very elastic.

How a radiologist's day changes

The most certain effect of AI is on what radiologists do, not how many are needed. Drafting and measurement shrink first, followed by primary interpretation. Consultation, procedures and new oversight work take up a growing share of the day.

What would change the forecast

Conditional probabilities from the simulation: if we observe X, how likely is meaningful oversupply?

Signals to be more pessimistic

  • Prospective, multi-site studies showing ≥15% real-world time savings from generative reporting by ~2030
  • FDA authorization of autonomous reads for any U.S. radiology exam class
  • CMS or major payers paying for AI-only reads, or malpractice safe-harbor laws
  • Per-capita imaging flattening or falling (utilization management, Medicare's 2033 trust-fund squeeze)
  • DR residency positions passing ~1,400 a year
  • Frontier AI reliably completing multi-day clinical reasoning tasks

Signals to be more optimistic

  • Real-world AI time savings staying in single digits
  • Autonomous-reading products stalling at FDA, liability or payment
  • CT/MRI volume growth staying at 3%+ a year
  • Screening (lung, opportunistic CT) scaling with radiologists in the loop
  • AI creating paid radiologist-led services: quantitative imaging, theranostics, AI governance
  • Attrition staying at post-COVID highs
Bottom line. On a 10-year horizon, radiology looks like a reasonable bet; its risks come late and sit mostly in a transformative-AI tail. To hedge, build skills in the tasks that are slower to automate (procedures and IR, consultation, breast intervention, informatics and AI governance), stay geographically flexible, and update each year on the signals above rather than on headlines.

How to read this forecast

This is a forecast in the sense used by forecasting tournaments: explicit probabilities, an outside view first, and named signposts for updating. Long-range numbers like " in 2055" are coarse. Treat them as "roughly two in five," not as precise measurements. Most of the spread comes from judgments about AI, regulation and future imaging use, and those judgments are listed and graded above.

The economic frame is the task-based model of automation: AI displaces labor from some tasks, raises demand through productivity, and reinstates labor through new tasks. The workforce baseline comes from the Neiman Institute's 2025–2055 projections of imaging utilization and radiologist supply, adjusted for CBO's 2026 lower-immigration demographic outlook.

The full report has every equation, validation check, sensitivity table and limitation. The repository has the Python model, which reproduces every number on this page in about 15 seconds.
